## Description

DermTrack is a web app a dermatology clinic gives its patients to track skin concerns between visits. A patient signs in, captures a selfie through YouCam's guided JS Camera Kit, and gets an AI Skin Analysis scoring 10 concerns — wrinkles, acne, oiliness, eye bags, dark circles, age spots, pores, texture, redness, radiance — each 0–100. Every concern surfaces 2–3 treatment options framed as "your provider may recommend," so nothing reads as app-generated medical advice. Picking a treatment triggers an AI Skin Simulation: a realistic, personalized preview of that concern improving, rendered at two calibrated intensities — 0.3 and 0.7 — rather than one exaggerated "after" shot, so the patient sees a plausible version of their own face partway and further along a treatment path. Every capture is saved as a visit, building a real history over time — a trend line graph per concern (with the visit photo on hover), a reverse-chronological visit list, and a printable "share with your provider" summary a patient can bring to their next real appointment.

For the consumer, the value is motivation and continuity: visible, personalized evidence that a routine is working (or isn't) is a much stronger nudge to stick with a treatment than a calendar reminder, and a running history means a patient walks into their next appointment with actual data instead of a vague "I think it's better?" For the clinic (the retail side), it's a low-cost way to stay present in a patient's routine between billable visits, reinforce adherence to treatments the provider already prescribed rather than losing patients to generic consumer skincare apps, and walk into follow-ups with an at-a-glance record of what was tried and what changed — all under the clinic's own name, not a third-party consumer brand.

## Inspiration

Dermatology patients rarely see day-to-day change, which is a big part of why treatment adherence is hard — nothing visibly rewards sticking with a routine for weeks. YouCam's pairing of Skin Analysis and Skin Simulation is a rare combination: real measurement plus a believable, personalized "here's what continuing this could look like." That felt like the right foundation for a tool a dermatology clinic could hand to a patient directly — not another consumer skin app that self-diagnoses and sells product, but something that reinforces the treatment plan the patient's actual provider already gave them.

## What it does

DermTrack is a dermatology-clinic-provided web app. A patient signs in (password or passwordless magic link), captures a selfie through YouCam's JS Camera Kit — which gives live feedback on lighting, pose, and framing before the shot is even taken — and runs it through YouCam's Skin Analysis across 10 concerns, each scored 0–100. For any concern, the patient sees 2–3 treatment options framed as "your provider may recommend," picks one, and gets a YouCam Skin Simulation projection at two intensities showing a realistic preview of that concern improving. Every capture becomes a "visit," so over time the patient builds a real visit history: a reverse-chronological list, a trend line graph per concern with a photo preview on hover, and a printable "share with your provider" summary for the next real appointment.

## How we built it

Next.js 14 (App Router, plain JavaScript) with Supabase for auth, Postgres, and Storage. Every YouCam call is proxied through our own `/api/youcam/*` routes so the API key never reaches the client. Photos never touch a public URL: the Storage bucket is private, the database stores only storage paths, and every path is resolved to a short-lived signed URL generated fresh at the point of use — a few minutes when handing it to YouCam, about an hour when rendering it in the UI. Because YouCam's own result URLs expire two hours after a task succeeds, every analysis mask and simulation output is downloaded and re-hosted into our bucket immediately after success. Analysis and Simulation use different, inconsistently-pluralized concern-key vocabularies for the same concepts (`eye_bag` vs. `eye_bags`, `dark_circle_v2` vs. `dark_circle`), so we built one canonical lookup table (`concernKeyMap.js`) that the rest of the app reads from, instead of hardcoding either API's strings more than once. A shared polling helper drives both long-running task types, with loading states that narrate what's actually happening ("Calling Skin Analysis…") rather than a generic spinner. Photos are validated and cropped client-side (`react-easy-crop` + canvas resize) before upload, so a bad framing or resolution issue never has to round-trip through a failed API call. The trend graph uses Chart.js via `react-chartjs-2`, with a custom external tooltip so hovering a data point shows that visit's actual photo. Styling is plain CSS on a small warm, clinical-but-soft design-token palette — no UI framework.

## Challenges we ran into

The Analysis/Simulation key mismatch was the easiest place to introduce a silent bug — a single typo swapping `dark_circle` and `dark_circle_v2` wouldn't throw an error, it would just quietly target the wrong concern, so we isolated it behind one mapping file and wrote tests specifically against it rather than trusting careful reading. The two-hour expiry on YouCam's result URLs, combined with this being health-adjacent photo data, meant we couldn't treat "never persist a resolvable link" as an afterthought — it had to be the rule from the first image upload, touching nearly every part of the pipeline (original selfie, masks, simulation outputs alike). Layering our own crop/validation step on top of whatever the JS Camera Kit already guides the user through, without it feeling like two disconnected steps, took some iteration. And keeping the polling UX honest was harder than it sounds — a multi-second Analysis or Simulation call needs a loading state that visibly shows the API working, or a demo (and a real user) just sees dead time.

## Accomplishments that we're proud of

A private-by-default image pipeline — row-level security scoped to `auth.uid()` on every table, a private storage bucket, path-only persistence, signed URLs generated fresh at request time — built in from the start for genuinely sensitive data, without it slowing the build down. The two most error-prone pieces of the integration (the concern-key map and the shared polling helper) are both covered by tests specifically because they're the spots where a bug is hardest to catch by eye. And the treatment-to-simulation loop feels clinically credible rather than gimmicky: provider-framed treatment options, then a two-intensity projection of that same concern, not a generic filter slapped on a selfie.

## What we learned

Isolating vocabulary drift between two related-but-inconsistent APIs behind one translation layer immediately — instead of patching call sites as mismatches surface — is worth doing on day one, not after the first silent bug. Designing around short-lived URLs from the very first upload is much easier than retrofitting it later; treating "never persist a resolvable link" as a hard rule up front avoided what would otherwise have been a late security scramble. Real, in-browser capture guidance (YouCam's JS Camera Kit) visibly cuts failed API calls compared to a plain file input, which is easy to underestimate until you compare the two side by side. And loading-state copy turned out to be a cheap, high-leverage way to make an API-driven product's technical depth visible to someone who can't see the network tab — a judge included.

## What's next for DermTrack

Letting each clinic customize its own treatment-recommendation config per concern instead of sharing one static config across every clinic. Supporting multiple photo angles per visit (front, left, right) for a fuller picture than a single selfie. A clinician-facing view into a patient's visit history and simulation choices, so the loop between the app and the actual provider visit closes instead of staying one-directional. HD-tier analysis for clinics with in-office capture hardware, alongside the current SD-only patient-facing flow. And visit-cadence reminders, so the trend graph fills in with real data over time instead of depending on the patient remembering to come back.
